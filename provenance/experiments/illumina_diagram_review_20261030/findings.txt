Illumina schematic review - 30 October 2026

What was done

Rechecked the previously cached 154 source catalogue records. Downloaded and
visually inspected 16 original Illumina workflow PNGs. Examined selected primary
paper and protocol passages to investigate specific comparisons and discrepancies.
Wrote six comparison records in manual_comparisons.json, identifying shared
features, changes, interpretation limits and evidence. These are manual research
annotations, not independently reviewed graph admissions or a model-run evaluation.

The catalogue audit and diagram download receipts preserve source URLs and SHA-256
hashes. Earlier failed urllib TLS downloads remain recorded. The successful curl
downloads used the system certificate store; certificate verification was not
disabled. Original transparent PNGs were preserved. Some needed compositing on
white to be readable in the inspection tool, which was a rendering issue rather
than missing scientific content.

1. Shared prefix and one changed branch: G&T-seq and scM&T-seq

Both diagrams show isolation/lysis, oligo(dT)-based physical separation of RNA
and DNA, and a Smart-seq2 transcriptome branch. The G&T diagram shows MDA on the
DNA branch; scM&T shows scBS-seq. The scM&T paper explicitly applies scBS-seq to
DNA purified with the G&T procedure. Physical separation allows DNA conversion
without exposing the transcriptome to bisulfite.

Useful graph correspondence:

  cell -> lysis -> separate RNA and DNA
                       | RNA -> Smart-seq2 branch
                       | DNA -> MDA branch [shown G&T implementation]
                              OR scBS-seq branch [scM&T]

This is a supported development relationship and a local branch replacement,
not merely two similarly named workflows. MDA is the displayed implementation,
not a universal restriction on G&T-seq. Other published G&T implementations can
use alternative genome amplification.

2. Same broad purpose, different physical carrier: Drop-seq and inDrop

The Drop-seq schematic says Pool all beads from droplets. The original paper
specifies breaking droplets, recovering RNA-bound beads and reverse transcription
in bulk. The inDrop schematic says UV primer release and Pool all droplets.
The original inDrop Figure 1 specifies photo-released primers and barcoding RT
inside droplets before their breakup.

Two collections of droplets may be pooled while their contents remain separated.
Therefore the phrase pooling cannot establish that molecules have been mixed or
that RT is performed in bulk. A useful comparison must retain the pooled object,
compartment persistence, primer attachment/release and encoding timing.

Similarity here does not establish a directed history edge between the methods.

3. Biological analyte versus sequenced proxy: RASL-seq and ClickSeq

RASL uses target RNA to align synthetic DNA probe pairs. Those probes are ligated,
released and amplified into the sequenced product. The original protocol maps
reads to intended ligation-product sequences. Reverse transcription is not a
universal requirement for an assay measuring RNA abundance.

ClickSeq instead makes RNA-derived cDNA with azido-modified ends, then joins a
modified adapter by click chemistry. Both include joining reactions, but they
join different molecular participants and generate different readout carriers.
The term ligation is therefore a retrieval clue, not a sufficient definition of
a shared mechanism or interchangeable stage.

4. Terminal chemistry determines selection: cP-RNA-seq and CIP-TAP

Both schematics include phosphatase treatment, ligation and library generation.
cP-RNA-seq shows alternative 3-prime end states, periodate treatment and T4 PNK.
CIP-TAP shows alternative 5-prime end states, including a cap, with TAP and
adapter attachment positioned in its selection sequence. This common reagent
does not make the full selection procedures equivalent.

The cP-RNA-seq paper's Limitations section adds a consequential qualification:
terminal 2-prime-O-methylated RNAs can resist periodate despite lacking cyclic
phosphate and can enter the library. Thus resistant RNA must not automatically
be described as exclusively cP-containing RNA. This is a measurement-interpretation
limit, not a reason to recover every buffer ingredient.

CIP-TAP in this comparison remains schematic-level; a complete primary-protocol
verification of CIP-TAP was not performed in this review.

5. An actual schematic-order discrepancy: RAD and ddRAD

The downloaded ddRAD diagram displays:
  restriction digestion -> add barcoded adapters -> second restriction digest.
The grouped RAD diagram similarly places Second restriction digest in ddRADseq
at the position occupied by shearing after initial adapter attachment.

The cited Peterson et al. 2012 paper instead specifies a simultaneous two-enzyme
digest, adapter ligation and precise size selection; the paper explicitly
describes eliminating random shearing/end repair. The local cached JATS paragraph
and the publisher's text agree. Its separate protocol likewise starts with a
double digest before ligation.

Resolution for the Peterson implementation: use the primary protocol's ordering
in the scientific graph and retain a note that literal transcription of the
Illumina schematic would give a different order. Do not claim all subsequent
ddRAD variants are identical. This discrepancy has an available primary-source
resolution; it is not an unexplained biological impossibility.

6. Reuse with a changed interface: ChIPmentation

The ChIPmentation diagram places Tn5 adapter insertion after immunoprecipitation.
The primary paper identifies bead-bound immunoprecipitated chromatin as the
substrate. The meaningful change is adapter insertion at that point and in that
material context, not the existence of Tn5 alone.

The grouped ChIP-seq/ChIP-exo/HT-ChIP diagram includes exonuclease digestion and
cannot supply a universal baseline for all members. Its first listed ChIP-seq
reference is a 1988 foundational ChIP study, not sequencing-based ChIP-seq.
A chronology built from the first listed reference would conflate component
history with assay history. A 2007 primary sequencing-based ChIP paper provides
a direct counterexample to interpreting 1988 as the sequencing assay's date.

Other confirmed catalogue/representation issues

- 24 of 153 titled records list multiple names. This includes aliases and
  genuine method families; it is not a count of 24 incorrect diagrams.
- 3--seq and 3prime-seq share the title 3'-Seq and exactly the same image URL.
  The separate 3seq card has a different diagram and targets degraded FFPE RNA;
  typography alone must not merge it with 3'-Seq.
- CaptureSeq delegates several library-construction transformations to one
  TruSeq stranded mRNA sample preparation block. Correspondence can be one to
  several operations and should not require identical numbers of boxes.
- Generic UMI/component entries and complete assay entries have different scope.
- Similar Nuc-Seq names describe RNA and DNA workflows in different cards.

What these findings imply for the extraction target

The diagrams remain a good default architecture scaffold. Required detail is
the detail needed to identify the transformed material, its important features,
the readout carrier, retained associations, branches and supported difference.
The review does not justify maximal experimental reconstruction for every assay.

A diagram extraction should be checked for both faithful transcription and
scientific agreement with its cited implementation. Those can diverge, as the
ddRAD example shows. The graph should identify the chosen implementation and
the source supporting its order, instead of silently combining incompatible
descriptions or accepting the diagram as infallible.

Matching needs explicit context and evidence rather than identical stage names:
  shared prefix/branch -> G&T/scM&T positive case
  preserved compartment -> Drop-seq/inDrop boundary case
  different readout carrier -> RASL/ClickSeq negative mechanism case
  reagent versus selection -> cP-RNA/CIP-TAP partial-match case
  source order -> RAD/ddRAD correction case
  substrate/interface placement -> ChIPmentation reuse case

No extraction prompts, runtime, frozen runs, existing workflow artifacts or
canonical atlas edges were changed by this research review. No new native model
run was launched. The next software evaluation can use these documented cases;
the present review itself does not establish that the harness passes them.
